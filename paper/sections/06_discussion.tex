\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What this paper claims.}
The construction is the contribution we are most confident of: a causal probabilistic
transformer exists, it is forced rather than chosen at every step where a choice looked
available, and next-token prediction in it is inference over a variable the model already had.
Weight tying, the language-model bias, the \textsc{[mask]}-style query and a
mixture-of-softmaxes readout are consequences of that, not additions to it. What we do not
claim is a perplexity win. Across the budgets we measure, the model is a factor of $\MBMinRatioGpt$ to
$\MBMaxRatioGpt$ behind a causal transformer on this corpus, and per parameter it gains less
than either baseline on every range where the comparison can be made. Where the exponents put
the cause is worth being exact about: on the one range where all three are measured, sharing
parameters across depth accounts for most of the shallow slope --- $\ExpTriTf$ against
$\ExpTriLp$ --- and the syntactic structure for the rest, $\ExpTriLp$ against $\ExpTriLow$. The structure costs something beyond the sharing,
and it is the part this model class is answerable for. That is a smaller gap than we reported
before and it is arrived at honestly; it is not a win, and we have tried not to write it as one.

\paragraph{The bottleneck is not where it looks.}
It is tempting to read the model as bottlenecked by its $\nlab$ discrete labels, and to expect a
gap that no amount of tuning removes. \Cref{prop:readout} says otherwise: the mean-field
readout's constraint is the ordinary softmax bottleneck \citep{yang2018breaking} at
$\nlab = d_{\text{model}}$, no worse in kind or degree than a transformer's own head at matched
width, and the exact readout escapes even that at zero parameter cost. The per-position state
is one point of $\Delta^{\nlab-1}$ against a transformer's $\R^{d_{\text{model}}}$ --- the same
width, with the simplex geometry as the genuine difference. What is left is the \emph{dynamics}:
whether attention-only iterations with tied parameters and bounded states can put as much usable
information into those $\nlab$ numbers as a transformer stack puts into $d_{\text{model}}$. Our
switch ladder is built to answer exactly that question rather than argue about it, and it puts
the cost squarely there: every difference in the \emph{static} description of the model --- tied
key and value, no query or output projection, relative positions --- is free, while the two that
concern what the state \emph{is} and how it moves are not.

Pricing each difference twice, rather than once, turns out to be what makes the ladder say
anything. The properties that are free to \emph{adopt} are not free to \emph{remove}: inside the
all-PT rung, restoring any one of the ten transformer properties makes the model worse, and four
of them break it outright (\Cref{sec:exp-switches}). A construction whose parts are individually
cheap and jointly load-bearing is not a transformer with substitutions, and the two columns
together are the only way we found to see that.

Two of our results sharpen that into something a successor should take seriously. The exact
readout is a strictly richer family than the mean-field one at zero parameter cost, and training
under it is a hundred perplexity \emph{worse} (\Cref{sec:exp3}); and more mean-field iterations
make the model worse, so at this scale the best version of it runs one
(\Cref{sec:exp-depth}). Both say the same thing from different directions --- that the limit
here is not what the family can express but what this objective and this optimiser can reach
inside it --- and both point away from expressiveness arguments and towards the optimisation of
the inference loop as the thing to fix next.

The factored-label experiment adds the one piece of evidence that cuts the other way, and it is
worth taking seriously for that reason. The exact readout's penalty is not a constant: it falls
from $52$ perplexity at $K=1$ to nothing at $K=4$ (\Cref{sec:exp-factored}). Read with the
paragraph above, that suggests the readout was not being punished for being richer but for
having nothing extra to be rich \emph{about} --- with one label variable the product of mixtures
is a single mixture, and splitting the variable is what gives it something to express. If that
is right, the two most promising directions in this paper are the same direction: factor the
label variable, and use the readout the graph actually prescribes.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters are the practical obstacle, and they are analysable.}
The most useful thing we can report to anyone who wants to build on this is that the causal
model's sensitivity to width is not diffuse. It has a mechanism --- a head temperature
calibrated for a belief distribution the model leaves in the first few hundred steps
(\Cref{sec:temperature}) --- and correcting it revives label-set sizes at which the source
parameterisation sits exactly on the unigram baseline. \citet{kuang2026scaling} reach a
comparable conclusion for the encoder by a different route, transporting maximal update
parametrisation \citep{yang2022tensor} to it and scaling to 0.4B parameters, where they report
the probabilistic transformer \emph{ahead} of a standard one at matched budget. Read together,
the two results say that the small-scale gaps reported for this model class have been at least
partly artefacts of tuning, and that the interesting question is which parameterisation makes
each variant transfer.

It is also worth saying which of this model's knobs turned out to matter, since a reader
inheriting seven of them where a transformer has one will want to know where to spend a budget.
\textbf{Four are decisive}: the head temperature (\Cref{sec:exp-width} --- the difference
between training and sitting on the unigram), the learning rate (twenty times the source's
value, and interacting with width), the Kruskal rank of the arc scores (better perplexity at an
eighth of the parameters), and the label-set size itself. \textbf{One is decisive in the wrong
direction}: the iteration count, where fewer is better (\Cref{sec:exp-depth}). \textbf{The
remaining two are unalike, and the difference is the useful part.} The $L_2$ coefficient on the
arc scores behaves like an ordinary regulariser: a genuine interior optimum at the value the
source already uses, gentle degradation below it, catastrophe two orders above. The
relative-position clip does not: it is a \emph{window}, with the model dead at $\clip = 0$ and
dead again at $\clip = 7$, and only two of the four values we ran inside it
(\Cref{sec:exp-knobs}). Both walls of that window, and the collapse at large $L_2$, are the same
failure as the head temperature --- the message-to-unary balance leaving the range in which the
label update is stable. That is the generalisation we would offer a successor: the quantity to
monitor while tuning this model is not perplexity but that balance, because perplexity is flat
right up to the point where it is $600$. These were measured at different budgets
and are an ordering rather than a ranking, but the ordering is the practical content of
\citet{kuang2026scaling}'s observation restated from the decoder side: what matters are the two
knobs that set the \emph{scale} of the messages and the one that sets their \emph{rank}, and
those are exactly the ones the source fixes by a rule that does not transfer.

\paragraph{The missing feed-forward block is missing, and the in-graph substitute does not
substitute.} Two of our experiments meet here and neither says this alone. The switch ladder
prices the feed-forward block at $15.1$ perplexity in an otherwise ordinary decoder --- the
largest cost of any single difference that is not a cliff. The global variables of
\citet{wu2023probabilistic}'s Appendix B.3 are the in-graph replacement for exactly that block,
and what they buy is not capacity: at a width where the model trains without them they are worth
nothing, and at a width where it barely does they change which parameterisations are stable
without making the model stable. In every cell of both grids the head posterior stays within
$7\,\%$ of uniform, including where its message exceeds the word unary. The one thing in that
experiment worth carrying forward is not about the head at all: a semi-orthogonal draw for
$\Bglob$ gives a seed standard deviation of $0.6$ where a Gaussian one gives $10.2$
(\Cref{sec:exp-ablations}). Maximal update parametrisation
\citep{yang2022tensor} assumes a Gaussian draw, so the draw is a place where the transfer
results this model class now rests on may not be neutral; what we can add is that on this
evidence its effect is on variance rather than on the mean, which is the axis that has cost this
project the most.
A variable whose posterior is uniform is sending a constant, and a constant is not a
feed-forward block. The mechanism behind the uniformity is a symmetry --- rows that begin close
together receive identical gradients and a shared $L_2$ keeps them there --- which is a
statement about the parameterisation and not about the construction, so we do not read it as a
proof that no in-graph feed-forward substitute can work. What we can say is that the one the
source proposes does not close the gap it was proposed for, that the gap is real and now
priced, and that closing it is the most concrete piece of unfinished business this model class
has.

\paragraph{A repair that leaves the graph.}
We report one uncomfortable thing plainly. The temperature correction that works best is a
standardisation of the head logits, and that is not a message through a factor: it is a
normalisation applied outside the update equations, and it forfeits the property that every
update is the centred gradient of a fixed per-slot free energy. The version that stays inside
the construction --- a temperature that tracks the belief norm, and so equals the source's
choice exactly at the uniform belief --- helps, but less. We take this as informative rather
than embarrassing. It prices one specific consequence of the discipline this model class
imposes, and it is the same price a transformer pays knowingly when it puts a layer
normalisation in front of its attention.

\paragraph{The interpretation is real and it is parameterisation-dependent.}
Probing trained models directly (\Cref{app:interpretability}) gives the clearest statement of
what this construction buys, and the clearest warning about how to read such statements. In the
configuration \Cref{sec:exp-width} selects, the head posterior attaches at a mean distance of
$11.1$ positions against $17.0$ for uniform attention --- a genuine preference for nearby heads
--- and the prior-to-posterior divergence at a slot correlates with the surprisal of the word
observed there at $r = 0.47$, which is the qualitative content of the construction's own
contraction bound. In the configuration our earlier work selected, the same probe finds the
attention collapsed onto the previous word and the correlation at $r = 0.16$. Same model class,
same corpus, opposite readings. Interpretability results from this family should be quoted with
the parameterisation that produced them, and we would not have known that without running the
probe twice.

\paragraph{Extensions.}
Three directions follow from what we measured rather than from what we hoped: factored labels
with the exact readout, which is the strongest graph-faithful head available and whose gain is
predicted to exist only there (\Cref{sec:exp-factored}); continuous or vector-valued label
variables, which dissolve the scale tension at the cost of the discrete interpretability that
is the point; and fixed-lag smoothing, a graded in-graph repair of the one evidence term the
frozen prefix drops. Whether any of them is worth its complexity is decided by measurement, and
we have tried to leave the measurements rather than the conclusions.
