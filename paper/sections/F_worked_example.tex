\section{Worked Example}
\label{app:worked-example}

% Wu & Tu's Appendix F holds case studies of the dependency structures their model infers. Ours
% holds the numeric example, which serves the same purpose: showing the reader what the model
% actually computes rather than asserting that it computes it. Every number below is asserted in
% `tests/test_07_worked_example.py` against an independently derived reference, so the appendix
% and the test suite cannot drift apart -- change one and the other fails.

The example is small enough to check by hand and is worked through by an independent reference
document, which is what makes reproducing it worth anything: these are not numbers this
implementation produced and then enshrined.

\paragraph{Setting.}
$\Vocab = \{\textit{the}, \textit{cat}, \textit{sat}, \textit{mat}\}$, two labels which we read
as $\{\textsc{n}, \textsc{v}\}$, one channel, $\lamZ = \lamH = \lamW = 1$, a
distance-insensitive arc score ($\clip = 0$), one inner round per observed slot and $\tau = 2$
in predictive mode. The factors are
%
\[
  \Sfac = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & -1.5 \\ 2 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}\!,\;\;
  \Tfac{} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\!,
\]
\[
  \bfac = (1,0,0,0), \qquad \rfac{} = (0, 1.5).
\]
%
The prefix is \emph{the cat sat} and the model is asked for the word at slot 4.

\paragraph{Observed pass.}
Each slot initialises from its own unary, $\QZ{}^{(0)} = \sigma(\Sfac_{w,\cdot})$, giving
$(.953,.047)$, $(.982,.018)$ and $(.018,.982)$; one round of the head and label updates against
the frozen prefix then gives the filtering marginals
%
\[
  \qbar{1} = (.818,.182), \quad \qbar{2} = (.957,.043),
\]
\vspace{-1.2em}
\[
  \qbar{3} = (.006,.994).
\]
%
The third is the verb, and the model has found it. Contracting these into arc scores by
\Cref{eq:contract} gives the cache the next slot reads,
$\Bfac{}_{1,\cdot} = (.365,.818)$, $\Bfac{}_{2,\cdot} = (.085,.957)$,
$\Bfac{}_{3,\cdot} = (1.987,.006)$ --- three numbers per position, and nothing else of the
prefix survives.

\paragraph{Predictive slot.}
The word variable is unclamped, so it initialises from its own unary factor:
$\QW{}^{(0)} = \sigma(\bfac) = (.475,.175,.175,.175)$, whence the query
$\tilde{s} = (1.063,-1.063)$ and $\QZ{}^{(0)} = (.893,.107)$. This is the \textsc{[mask]}
embedding, and it was not designed --- it is what the initialisation rule of every other
variable produces for this one. Two rounds of the inner loop over the head domain
$\{\textsc{root}, 1, 2, 3\}$:
%
\begin{align*}
  F^{(1)} &= (.160,.413,.178,1.776) \\
  \QH{}{(1)} &= (.120,.154,.122,.604) \\
  \QZ{}^{(1)} &= (.951,.049) \\[3pt]
  F^{(2)} &= (.074,.387,.128,1.890) \\
  \QH{}{(2)} &= (.104,.143,.110,.642) \\
  \QZ{}^{(2)} &= (.956,.044)
\end{align*}
%
The slot attends mostly to position 3, the verb, which is the head a noun object should choose.
The word update then \emph{is} the output layer: logits
$(2.368, 1.824, -1.824, 1.824)$ and
%
\[
  \hat{p}(\Wv{4}) = (.460,\, .267,\, .007,\, .267)
\]
%
over $(\textit{the},\textit{cat},\textit{sat},\textit{mat})$ --- the verb correctly almost
excluded, and \emph{mat} and \emph{cat} tied because nothing in this two-label toy
distinguishes two nouns.

\paragraph{Prior against posterior, on one slot.}
Clamping $\Wv{4} = \textit{mat}$ and running the observed step of the same slot sharpens the
label belief --- entropy $0.180 \to 0.040$ nats --- and moves the attention on the verb from
$.642$ to $.663$. That is the prior-to-posterior behaviour the construction predicts: observing
the word makes the slot both more certain of its label and more sure of its head.

\paragraph{The exact readout on the same slot.}
Replacing the mean-field readout by \Cref{eq:exact-readout} on the identical parameters gives
$\hat{p}(\Wv{4}) = (.365,.215,.206,.215)$. It is visibly flatter, and in particular it does not
almost exclude the verb. This is the Jensen gap made concrete: the mean-field readout commits to
a single soft label and then reads the vocabulary off it, while the exact readout mixes over
labels, so a word compatible with a label the slot considers unlikely retains more mass. Which
behaviour is preferable is an empirical question and \Cref{sec:exp3} is where we answer it; the
example only shows that the two are genuinely different objects rather than two computations of
one.

\section{What the Latent Variables Learn}
\label{app:interpretability}

% The analogue of Wu & Tu's Appendix F, which reports case studies of the dependency structures
% their encoder induces -- and reports honestly that they are only partially consistent with
% human intuition. This model is proposed because its latent variables mean something, so a
% paper that reported only perplexity would have left its central claim untested. All three
% probes read a trained checkpoint with no further training
% (`python -m experiments.interpretability <ckpt>`).

This model class is proposed because its latent variables mean something. Reporting only
perplexity would leave that untested, so we probe a trained model directly.

\paragraph{What each label claims.}
The word--label factor is the only place a word touches the graph, so the words a label explains
are those with the largest $\Sfac_{w,a}$. Ranking raw scores is dominated by words with large
rows, so we rank by the contrastive score
$\Sfac_{w,a} - \max_{a' \neq a}\Sfac_{w,a'}$ --- what this label claims against the competition.
Of $\nlab = 32$ labels, several are recognisably syntactic:

\begin{center}\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
label & top words by contrastive score \\
\midrule
$z{=}6$  & \textit{reconsider condemn leave lend hire notify restore} \\
$z{=}30$ & \textit{prefers manages chooses earns requires bears helps} \\
$z{=}13$ & \textit{to affects between from until thus indeed besides} \\
$z{=}19$ & \textit{controlled punishable bolstered funded headed owned} \\
$z{=}20$ & \textit{henderson cook jr.\ fisher sr.\ robertson generale} \\
$z{=}26$ & \textit{pence marks miles equaling tons} \\
$z{=}22$ & \textit{speculation deficits view sums notion evidence} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Reading them in order: bare infinitives; third-person singular present verbs; prepositions and
subordinators; past participles; person names; units of measure; abstract nouns. Nothing
supervised these categories --- they are what maximising the likelihood through a
$32$-value bottleneck produced. Not every label is interpretable, and we do not claim otherwise;
roughly a third of them resist any reading we can offer, which matches what
\citet{wu2023probabilistic} report for the encoder.

\paragraph{Where the head posterior looks.}
The head variable is the model's dependency attachment, and this is where the configuration
matters more than anything else in this paper. We probed two trained models: the one this
project's earlier work selected ($\nlab=32$, the source head temperature, damped) and the one
\Cref{sec:exp-width} selects ($\nlab=96$, $\krank=12$, standardised temperature, undamped).

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
 & earlier & selected \\
\midrule
\textsc{root} mass                & $0.486$ & $0.277$ \\
mean attachment distance          & $1.05$  & $11.06$ \\
mass at distance 1                & $0.976$ & $0.327$ \\
mass beyond distance 3            & $0.001$ & $0.497$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The earlier model had \emph{not} learned attachment: it put $0.976$ of its positional mass at
distance one and essentially nothing past three, which is a bigram attachment with a large sink.
The selected model has. Its mean attachment distance is $11.1$ against $17.0$ for uniform
attention over the same prefixes, it is $5.2$ times uniform at distance one, and it still places
half its mass beyond distance three. That is what a preference for nearby heads looks like when
it is a preference rather than a collapse.

\textbf{We report both because the difference is the point.} The same probe on the same corpus
gives opposite answers about whether this model class learns dependency structure, and what
separates them is the head temperature and the arc-score rank --- the two things
\Cref{sec:exp-width} is about. A reader who takes an interpretability result from a probabilistic
transformer without knowing which parameterisation produced it is not being told very much.
\Cref{fig:attachment} draws the selected model's profile against the uniform reference.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_attachment.png}
\caption{Where the head posterior attaches in the configuration of \Cref{sec:exp-width}
($\nlab=96$, $\krank=12$), against what uniform attention over the same prefixes would give.
The posterior is $5.2$ times uniform at distance one and falls below it further out, but half

The steep part covers exactly the distances the clipped table gives their own arc scores

\end{figure}

\paragraph{Prior against posterior.}
Part~IV's contraction bound predicts that the divergence between a slot's predictive and observed
beliefs is small on predictable tokens and grows with the surprisal of the word actually
observed. Measured over $4{,}032$ slots in each model, the direction the theory predicts is
present in both and its strength separates them exactly as the attachment probe does: in the
selected configuration the correlation is $r = 0.47$, with mean divergence $4.07$ on the most
predictable quartile of tokens against $6.56$ on the most surprising, while in the earlier one
it is $r = 0.16$, $1.98$ against $2.58$. The bound claims a direction and not a strength, so we
read nothing into the magnitudes beyond the same conclusion the rest of this section reaches ---
that the parameterisation decides how much of the predicted structure is there to find.

The label lexicon above is from the $\nlab=32$ model, where $32$ labels over a $10{,}000$-word
vocabulary give each label enough words to be readable. At $\nlab=96$ the same probe finds
fewer clean categories and more fragments --- adverbs (\textit{hastily, mildly, extremely,
widely}), gerunds (\textit{completing, entering, joining, contemplating}), units
(\textit{bottle, par, pound, capita, bushel}) and time nouns (\textit{century, decade, phase,
1970s}) survive, but a majority resist reading. Interpretability and capacity pull in opposite
directions along the width axis, and we did not find a width where both are best.
