Transformers are usually designed rather than derived. \citet{wu2023probabilistic} take the
opposite route: they define a conditional random field over the discrete latent labels of the
words in a sentence and over the dependency arcs between them, perform approximate inference
with mean field variational inference, and observe that the resulting computation graph closely
resembles a transformer encoder. Self-attention appears there not as a component that was
chosen, but as the posterior distribution over syntactic dependency heads. The model is not an
attempt to improve on transformers, but an account of what one of them might be doing.

That account stops at the encoder. It is trained with masked language modelling, whereas
transformers are now predominantly used autoregressively. \citet{wu2023probabilistic} name a
decoder as an open extension and do not pursue it. We ask whether the probabilistic account
survives the move to generation, or whether it was a property of the encoder all along.

Masking the dependency factors so that a head may only point backwards is not enough. Under a
single global energy the marginals at two positions share a normalising constant, so a factor
coupling them reaches the earlier one whenever it reaches the later one; masking changes which
configurations score highly, not that the survivors are normalised jointly. Causality, a single
global energy, and genuine contextual dependence cannot hold together, and the first and third
are what the model is for. We therefore replace the single field with a directed chain of
conditional random fields, one per position, in which the prefix posteriors enter as a frozen
condition, so that the anti-causal contribution is never formed rather than formed and deleted.

A second problem has no counterpart in the encoder: a model that predicts words needs a
distribution over the vocabulary. Projecting the label posterior through a matrix into
vocabulary space would add a component corresponding to no factor, so the prediction would leave
the model without traversing the graph. No new component is needed. The word is already a
variable of the graph, merely observed, and word--label compatibility is already a factor of it;
unclamping the variable turns that factor into a language model head. Properties that are
usually stipulated then follow: the input and output word embeddings are the same matrix, since
one factor mediates both directions; the output bias is the unary term on the word variable; and
encoding and decoding are two conditioning patterns of one model rather than two models.

Both results are refusals, and they refuse for the same reason: the factor graph is the model,
and it constrains what may be added to it. There is also a dividend. Conditioned on its prefix,
the subgraph at a single position is a star centred on the label variable, hence a tree, so
inference there is exact and the readout is a mixture of softmaxes in closed form, computed
along the sequence by a single causal scan that leaves the layer-parallel schedule intact.

Empirically we ask whether syntactic structure pays for itself. A comparison against a standard
decoder cannot settle this, because our model differs from one in two ways at once: it reuses a
single parameter set across inference iterations, and it has a syntactic factor graph. A looped
transformer, in which one block is applied repeatedly with shared weights, has the first without
the second, so the comparison against it is the one that isolates structure. Following
\citet{wu2023probabilistic}, our aim is not to compete with transformers on perplexity; a gap is
expected, since all predictive information passes through the label variables and the readout
