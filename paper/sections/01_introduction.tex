\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\citet{wu2023probabilistic} derive a transformer encoder from a probabilistic model. They define
a conditional random field (CRF) over latent syntactic labels of words and dependency arcs
between them, run mean-field variational inference (MFVI), and find that the resulting
computation graph closely resembles a transformer encoder \citep{vaswani2017attention}, with
self-attention as the posterior over dependency heads. The model is meant to explain what a
transformer might compute, not to improve on it. That account covers only the encoder, while
most transformers today are autoregressive decoders. We ask whether it survives the move to
generation, and what it costs.

A causal mask on the dependency factors is not enough. Under a single smooth energy every
coupling is two-sided, so masking one direction removes the other as well: causality, a single
global energy and contextual dependence cannot hold together (\Cref{thm:trilemma}). We give up
the single energy and use a directed chain of CRFs, one per position, in which the prefix
posteriors enter as a frozen condition, so the anti-causal term is never formed.

A decoder also needs a distribution over the vocabulary, and a projection into the vocabulary
would have no factor behind it. The word, however, is already a variable of the graph, only
observed; unclamping it turns the word--label factor into a language-model head. Tied input and
output embeddings follow, and so does the output bias. Encoding and decoding become two
conditioning patterns of one model. Given the prefix, the subgraph at each position is a tree,
so exact inference gives a mixture-of-softmaxes readout in closed form. The mean-field readout
has the softmax bottleneck of a transformer's head at matched width, and the exact readout
escapes it at no parameter cost (\Cref{prop:readout}).

Our model, the causal probabilistic transformer (causal PT), differs from a standard decoder in
two ways at once: it shares its parameters across inference iterations, and it has a syntactic
factor graph. A looped transformer shares weights without the structure, so comparing with it
isolates structure, and a set of ten single-difference ablations (the switch ladder) measures
what each difference costs on its own. Much of the model's sensitivity to width comes from the
attention temperature of Wu and Tu, which is calibrated only at the uniform belief. Correcting
it lets larger label sets train where they would otherwise collapse to the unigram
distribution. The best model reaches a validation perplexity of $\PTbestVal$ on the Penn
Treebank, against $445.2$ for a bigram model. Across sizes its perplexity is $\MBMinRatioGpt$
to $\MBMaxRatioGpt$ times that of a causal transformer interpolated to the same non-embedding
parameter count. Without supervision, some
labels of a small model align with syntactic categories, and in a wider model the heads prefer
nearby words (mean attachment distance $11$ against $17$ under uniform attention); how much of
either appears depends on the parameterisation (\Cref{app:interpretability}).
