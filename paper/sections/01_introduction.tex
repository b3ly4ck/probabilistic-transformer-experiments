Transformers are usually designed rather than derived. \citet{wu2023probabilistic} take the
opposite route: they define a conditional random field over the discrete latent labels of the
words in a sentence and over the dependency arcs between them, perform approximate inference
with mean field variational inference, and observe that the resulting computation graph closely
resembles a transformer encoder. Self-attention appears there not as a component that was
chosen, but as the posterior distribution over syntactic dependency heads; the attention weights
are computed by the inference procedure rather than parameterised directly. The model is
therefore not an attempt to improve on transformers but an account of what one of them might be
doing.

That account stops at the encoder. The model is trained with masked language modelling and
produces contextual representations of words that are all visible at once, whereas the setting
in which transformers are now predominantly used is autoregressive: each token is predicted from
its prefix alone. \citet{wu2023probabilistic} name a decoder as an open extension of their work
and do not pursue it. The question this paper asks is whether the probabilistic account survives
the move to generation, or whether it was a property of the encoder all along.

The obvious route does not work. One would like to mask the ternary factors so that a
dependency head may only point backwards, leaving everything else intact. This is not enough,
and the reason is the partition function: in a conditional random field with a single global
energy, a factor coupling two positions contributes to the marginal at the earlier one whenever
it contributes to the marginal at the later one, because both marginals are obtained from the
same normalising constant. Masking changes which configurations score highly; it does not stop
the surviving configurations from being normalised jointly. Causality, a single global energy
function, and any genuine dependence of a representation on its context cannot hold together.
Since the first and the third are what the model is for, the second is what we give up. In place
of one field over the sentence we define a directed chain of conditional random fields, one per
position, in which the posteriors over the prefix enter the next step as a frozen condition. The
anti-causal contribution is then never formed, rather than formed and deleted --- a distinction
that is easy to lose in implementation and that we test for directly.

A second problem has no counterpart in the encoder. A model that predicts words needs a
distribution over the vocabulary, and a conditional random field over labels and arcs does not
obviously provide one. The natural first attempt is to project the posterior over labels through
a matrix into vocabulary space, but such a matrix corresponds to no factor in the graph: the
prediction would leave the model through a component the posterior never reaches, and what
remained would be a conditional random field with a neural head attached to it. Our resolution
is that no new component is needed. The word is already a variable of the graph and the
word--label compatibility is already a factor of it; in the encoder that variable is simply
observed. Unclamping it turns the existing factor into a language model head, and the
distribution over the next word becomes the posterior over a variable the model always had.

Several properties then follow that are usually stipulated. The input and output word
embeddings are the same matrix, because one factor mediates both directions and there is no
second matrix to untie; tying is not a regularisation choice here but a property of the
construction, and untying would describe a different model. The bias of the output layer is the
unary term on the word variable. Encoding and decoding are not two models but two conditioning
patterns of one, differing only in whether the word variables are observed. We regard this as
the more interesting half of the paper, and we present it in a form intended to be checked.

Both of our main results are refusals, and they refuse for the same reason: the factor graph is
the model, and it constrains what may be added to it. One may not keep a single global energy
and be causal; one may not add an output matrix that names no factor. Taking the first seriously
forces the chain of conditional fields, and taking the second seriously forces the tied
embeddings, the output bias and the identity of the two directions. There is also a practical
dividend. Conditioned on its prefix, the subgraph at a single position is a star centred on the
label variable and therefore a tree, so sum-product inference is exact and the readout is
available in closed form as a mixture of softmaxes. Along the sequence it reduces to one causal
prefix log-sum-exp, computed by a single scan that leaves the layer-parallel schedule intact.

Empirically we ask whether syntactic structure pays for itself. This cannot be settled by a
comparison against a standard decoder alone, because our model differs from one in two ways at
once: it reuses a single parameter set across inference iterations, and it has a syntactic
factor graph. A looped transformer, in which one block is applied repeatedly with shared
weights, has the first property without the second, so the comparison against it is the one that
isolates the effect of structure. We report both. Our aim, following \citet{wu2023probabilistic},
is not to compete with transformers on perplexity; a gap is expected, since all predictive
information in our model passes through the label variables and the readout inherits their rank.
