Two corpora are used, both at the word level, and both reach every model through one reader and
one evaluation protocol. Penn Treebank is where all tuning happens; WikiText-2 is only the
transfer test of \Cref{sec:exp-scaling}. The entire tokenisation policy is one shared function,
written once rather than once per corpus, and that is deliberate rather than tidy: two copies of
the same few lines drift apart the first time one of them is touched, and the drift would then
be reported as the transfer result. The counts below were measured on the exact token streams
the runs consume; \Cref{tab:datasets} collects them.

\paragraph{Penn Treebank.}
We use the Penn Treebank \citep{marcus1993building} in the word-level preprocessing of
Mikolov's language-modelling release: lower-cased, every number replaced by the single token
\texttt{N}, out-of-vocabulary words already replaced by \texttt{<unk>} in the distributed files,
punctuation removed, and one sentence per line.
\NOTE{\texttt{refs.bib} carries no verified Mikolov entry, so this attribution and the
``10{,}000 types'' convention below are stated without a citation; add one before the
camera-ready or drop the two attributions.}
We take those files as they come, restoring and filtering nothing, and the preprocessing is
visible in the stream we measure: the only type carrying an upper-case letter is \texttt{N}
itself, which accounts for 32{,}481 of the 929{,}589 training tokens; only 46 of the training
types contain a digit at all, and those are compounds, decades and ordinals --- \emph{10-year},
\emph{1920s}, \emph{19th} --- never a bare numeral; and the only types containing neither a
letter nor a digit are \texttt{\#}, \texttt{\$},
\texttt{\&}, the apostrophe and two escaped asterisks. The reader appends \texttt{<eos>} to
every line, which turns the sentence boundary into a token the model has to predict rather than
a segmentation handed to it: 42{,}068 training sentences, 3{,}370 in validation and 3{,}761 in
test, so \texttt{<eos>} is $4.57\%$ of the validation tokens. The vocabulary is built from the
training split alone and comes to $9{,}999$ word types plus \texttt{<eos>}, the $10{,}000$ of
the standard setting. Because it is built from training text, no
evaluation token can be unseen --- we measured zero validation and zero test types absent from
train --- so the \texttt{<unk>} fallback in the reader never fires and the unigram baseline
below needs no smoothing.

\paragraph{WikiText-2.}
The second corpus is the word-level release of \citet{merity2017pointer}, the
\texttt{wikitext-2-v1} files rather than the \texttt{-raw-} release, which carries a different
preprocessing and is not interchangeable with them; the word-level files are the ones already
mapped to a closed vocabulary, and in them \texttt{<unk>} accounts for $54{,}625$ of the
training tokens. They are read by the same function as PTB, with the same whitespace
split, the same single \texttt{<eos>} per line and the same vocabulary construction from the
training split. Its raw files keep the article structure of the source: of the 3{,}760
validation lines, 1{,}299 are blank and 620 are headings, nested up to five levels deep and
written as \texttt{= Heading =}, \texttt{= = Sub = =} and so on. The standard protocol scores
both as they are, splitting on whitespace and appending one \texttt{<eos>} per line, blank lines
included, so that a blank line contributes a bare \texttt{<eos>}; on the validation split the
blank lines are $0.60\%$ of the tokens and the heading lines $2.37\%$. We keep that convention
unchanged. Filtering the headings would lower the token count and change the denominator of the
perplexity, which is the one thing a transfer test cannot afford, since it would leave our
number incomparable to every published one.
\NOTE{the ``scores them as they are'' convention is taken from the reference word-level
readers, not from a statement in \citet{merity2017pointer}; check before the camera-ready.}
The vocabulary is again closed by construction, with zero validation and zero test types absent
from train, and comes to $33{,}278$ types counting \texttt{<eos>}.

\paragraph{Batching.}
The tokens of a split are one \texttt{<eos>}-joined stream, cut into fixed-length blocks of
$\seqlen = 64$ tokens --- the nanoGPT convention \citep{karpathy2022nanogpt} --- and every model
sees exactly the same blocks in exactly the same order. Training draws blocks at uniformly
random offsets into the stream; evaluation, described below, does not. A block boundary is
therefore not a sentence boundary: a block generally starts mid-sentence, and its slot $0$ is a
token to be predicted from no prefix at all --- the slot that the protocol below drops.

\paragraph{Slot alignment.}
The causal PT emits $\text{logits}[:,t]$ predicting $w_t$ from $w_{\prefix{t}}$
(\Cref{sec:output}), so on a block of $\seqlen$ tokens it scores all $\seqlen$ of them,
including $w_0$ from \textsc{root} alone --- the first word has a proper distribution, which is
precisely why the model needs no beginning-of-sequence token. A causal transformer trained the
usual way consumes $w_0,\dots,w_{\seqlen-2}$ and predicts $w_1,\dots,w_{\seqlen-1}$: $\seqlen-1$
scored tokens. Averaging the two would compare different token sets, and the extra slot is a
first-word unigram guess that the baseline never has to make. We align them on the baseline's
side: the transformer wrapper runs the backbone on $w_{0:\seqlen-2}$ and shifts its logits right
by one slot, filling slot $0$ with zeros, so that it too obeys the convention
``$\text{logits}[:,t]$ predicts $w_t$''; both models then drop slot $0$ (\texttt{ignore\_first}
$=1$) in training and in evaluation alike, so neither receives gradient from a slot the other
cannot predict. The zero-filled slot is never scored, and the wrapper's loss asserts this rather
than assuming it --- scoring it would grade the baseline on a slot it does not emit. What both
models are asked for is thus $p(w_t \mid w_{\prefix{t}})$ for $t = 1,\dots,\seqlen-1$: $63$
scored tokens per block, $72{,}576$ on PTB validation and $214{,}200$ on WikiText-2 validation.

\paragraph{Evaluation determinism.}
The loop walks fixed, non-overlapping blocks in corpus order over the whole split, in batches of
$16$, and samples nothing: a perplexity that moved with the seed could not be compared
across runs or against the baseline. The trailing tokens that do not fill a block are dropped
--- $53$, $32$ and $62$ tokens on the three PTB splits and $52$, $46$ and $1$ on WikiText-2, at
worst $0.08\%$ of a split. The reported perplexity is the
exponential of the total negative log-likelihood over the scored tokens divided by their number,
a corpus-level average rather than a mean of per-block perplexities. The training perplexity we
trace alongside it is measured the same deterministic way, on a fixed $320$-block slice of the
training split.

\paragraph{Unigram baselines.}
The reference of \Cref{sec:tasks} is the maximum-likelihood unigram of the training split, and
it is scored through the loop above rather than in closed form, so that its denominator is the
same $63$ tokens per block as every model's. It is
$688.82$ on PTB validation and $964.82$ on WikiText-2 validation; including slot $0$ instead of
dropping it gives $687.45$ and $965.37$, a difference of at most $0.2\%$ that falls on opposite
sides on the two corpora, which is the entire effect of one token in sixty-four. We state this
reference explicitly because it has already caught a failure in this project: an earlier
implementation reached a validation perplexity of $664$ against a unigram baseline of $687$ and
the gap was read as learning until the samples were inspected. A model that does not clear the
unigram number by a wide margin has not learned to use context, whatever its loss curve looks
like.
\NOTE{the two WikiText-2 unigram figures were measured on 2026-09-08 on the release fetched by
\texttt{scripts/get\_wikitext2.sh} and still need their row in the run log of
\texttt{experiments/exp8\_transfer}; the two PTB figures already have one.}

\paragraph{WikiText-2 is not a second tuning corpus.}
Every hyperparameter --- learning rate and schedule, width, iteration count, temperature and
damping --- is selected on PTB and carried to WikiText-2 unchanged; no grid is rerun there. That
is what makes \Cref{sec:exp-scaling} a transfer test rather than a second experiment. Two
things change at once and we state them
rather than control them away: the vocabulary grows from $10{,}000$ to $33{,}278$ types and the
training set from $0.93$M to $2.09$M tokens, so the parameter count moves even at fixed $\nlab$,
because the word--label factor is $|\Vocab| \times \nlab$. That is unavoidable --- it is what
changing corpus means for a model whose parameters are almost all $\Sfac$ --- and it is why the
WikiText-2 comparison is always against baselines run on the same corpus through the same loop,
never against the PTB numbers. The readout constraint of \Cref{sec:cmp-readout} tightens for
the same reason: with $3.3$ times the vocabulary to place in an affine family of dimension
$\nlab$, the bound of \Cref{prop:readout} binds harder on WikiText-2 than on PTB. It binds on a
transformer's own output head in the same way, at $m = \nlab$, which is exactly why the
comparison stays within the corpus --- but a weaker absolute number on WikiText-2 than on PTB
has that confound in it.

Finally, we do not use WikiText-103, for the reason already given in \Cref{sec:tasks}.
