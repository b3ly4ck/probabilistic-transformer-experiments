\subsection{Tasks and Datasets}
\label{sec:tasks}

We train language models on the Penn Treebank \citep{marcus1993building} in the standard
word-level preprocessing (10{,}000 types; 929{,}589 / 73{,}760 / 82{,}430 tokens) and, as a
transfer test only, on WikiText-2 \citep{merity2017pointer} (33{,}278 types; 2{,}088{,}628 /
217{,}646 / 245{,}569 tokens). Both are read through one code path, with an \texttt{<eos>} token
per line and fixed 64-token blocks cut from the joined stream, so that a difference between the
two corpora cannot be a difference between two tokenisers (\Cref{app:datasets}). The reference
every number is read against is the maximum-likelihood unigram of the training split, scored on
the identical token set: \textbf{688.8} on PTB and \textbf{964.8} on WikiText-2.

\textbf{Every hyperparameter is selected on PTB and transferred to WikiText-2 unchanged.} This
follows the advice of \citet{kuang2026scaling}, whose method for transferring hyperparameters
across model sizes is explicitly not claimed to hold when the corpus changes as well; a
WikiText-2 number produced by a fresh grid search would say nothing about transfer, which is
the property under test.

We deliberately do not use WikiText-103. \citet{wu2023probabilistic} report that the encoder
model underperforms transformers beyond roughly $10^5$ sentences, suspected to be the absence
of a feed-forward structure; running there would test a known failure mode of the encoder
rather than the causal extension.

\subsection{Settings}
\label{sec:settings}

One training loop is shared by every model in this paper, and model-specific behaviour is
reached only through a three-method interface (the loss on a scored token set, an $L_2$
regulariser on the ternary scores which a transformer returns as zero, and a parameter count).
A change that helped one model and not the others would void the comparison, so there is
nothing in the loop that can branch on model type. AdamW, $\beta=(0.9,0.999)$, weight decay
$1.4\times10^{-6}$, gradient clipping at 1.0, 100 warmup steps then cosine decay to $0.1\times$,
$L_2$ on the arc scores $5\times10^{-4}$ (all from \citealp{wu2023probabilistic}, Table 2),
batches of 16 blocks of 64 tokens, 15{,}000 steps. Dropout is 0 for every model: its placement
inside the update equations of a probabilistic transformer is unspecified by the source and any
choice would cross the ``no maps'' constraint of \Cref{sec:variants}, so the baselines run
without it too.

\paragraph{Scored tokens.}
The causal PT emits $\text{logits}[t]$ predicting $w_t$ from $w_{<t}$, including $w_0$ from
\textsc{root} alone; a conventionally trained decoder emits $n-1$ predictions. We wrap the
baseline so that both answer $p(w_t \mid w_{<t})$ and drop slot 0 from every average, which
makes the two token sets identical rather than merely comparable; the baseline's loss asserts
that slot 0 is never scored. Evaluation is deterministic: fixed non-overlapping blocks in
corpus order over the whole split.

\paragraph{Checkpoint selection.}
Every model is scored at its best-validation checkpoint, chosen inside the shared loop, and
test perplexity is measured on that same checkpoint. This matters at this scale: PTB has under
a million training tokens, so 15{,}000 steps is 16.5 epochs and the larger baselines reach
their validation optimum long before the end --- a 1.2M-parameter transformer at step 5{,}000
against 15{,}000 for the causal PT. Scoring every model at the last step would grade some of
them past their optimum, and scoring each at its own best step is the standard protocol.

\paragraph{Learning rate.}
Each baseline point is the better of two learning rates ($10^{-3}$ and $3\times10^{-3}$), and
each rung of the switch ladder of \Cref{sec:exp-switches} the best of four. The causal PT
runs at $2\times10^{-2}$, twenty times the value in the source's Table 2; that this is
necessary, and that it interacts with the label-set size, is itself a finding
(\Cref{sec:exp-width}). A comparison in which one model is tuned and the other is not measures
the tuning.

\paragraph{Which readout, and why not the other one.}
\Cref{sec:inference} makes the exact readout the mainline of the \emph{construction}: it is what
sum--product on the slot tree gives, it costs no parameters, and it is the form the derivation
distinguishes. Every causal PT number in this section is nonetheless produced under the
\emph{mean-field} readout, and saying so here rather than leaving it to be inferred matters,
because the two are not interchangeable --- \Cref{sec:exp3} is the measurement of how far from
interchangeable. The reason is the order the work happened in: the scaling, width, rank and
switch grids were run before that measurement existed, and re-running them under the exact
readout is a comparison of trained models, not a re-scoring, so it is a second full sweep rather
than an evaluation pass. It is the one experiment in this paper whose absence is a matter of
GPU-hours rather than of design, and \Cref{sec:exp3} reports what has finished of it.

\paragraph{Two parameter axes, both reported.}
Tying is forced (\Cref{sec:output}), so almost all of the causal PT's parameters sit in
$\Sfac \in \R^{|\Vocab|\times\nlab}$: at $\nlab = 32$ that is 330{,}000 embedding parameters
against 16{,}448 for everything else. A transformer at $d_{\text{model}} = 32$ carries 322{,}048
and 50{,}880. Reporting only the non-embedding count flatters the causal PT by a large factor;
reporting only the total hides that its computational parameters are few. We plot both, and
state which axis any given claim is made on.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The order below is the order the questions have to be answered in. First, whether the
implementation is the model (\Cref{sec:exp-correctness}) and whether its numbers are
measurements at all (\Cref{sec:exp-repro}) --- the second of those changes how everything after
it may be read. Then the comparison the model exists to support: how it scales against a causal
transformer and a looped one (\Cref{sec:exp-scaling}), and what it costs at matched budget.
Then four questions about the model's own parameterisation --- its width
(\Cref{sec:exp-width}), its initialisation and the global variables
(\Cref{sec:exp-ablations}), the two constants with no gentle regime (\Cref{sec:exp-knobs}), and
its inference depth (\Cref{sec:exp-depth}). Last, two questions internal to the construction:
which readout to use (\Cref{sec:exp3}) and whether factoring the label variable buys anything
(\Cref{sec:exp-factored}), and whether any of it transfers to a second corpus
(\Cref{sec:exp-wt2}). The switch ladder (\Cref{sec:exp-switches}) sits between the two groups
because it is what turns ``worse than a transformer'' into a statement about named components.

\paragraph{Correctness of the implementation.}
\label{sec:exp-correctness}
Two checks test the derivation rather than the code and belong here; the rest are in
\Cref{app:validation}. The mean-field free energy is non-increasing along the inner loop on a
toy graph, and the check carries a mutation test --- a sign-flipped head update fails it while
every shape, normalisation and causality check passes unchanged. And on a single slot the graph
is a tree, so \Cref{eq:exact-readout} must agree with brute-force enumeration of the slot joint:
it does, to $6.7\times10^{-16}$ in float64 (\Cref{app:validation}). The same enumeration agrees
with a global variable folded in as an extra leaf, and the \texttt{logcumsumexp} scan agrees
with slot-by-slot assembly for $\clip \in \{0,1,2,4,9\}$; both are asserted at $10^{-12}$. The worked example of the output-mechanism note reproduces digit for
digit. The factored-label variant is checked the same way: at $K=2$, an enumeration over
$(\Wv{t}, \Zv{t}^{(1)}, \Zv{t}^{(2)}, \Hv{t}{1..4})$ written from the raw parameters agrees with
its closed form to $10^{-10}$, and setting $K=1$ reproduces the flat decoder to $6.9\times
10^{-18}$.

\paragraph{A methodological finding first: variance.}
\label{sec:exp-repro}
A configuration recorded at validation perplexity 430.04 in our own earlier work was re-run
here on the same code, the same seed and the same data order, and landed at 644.98. Three arms
separate the possible causes and they agree to every printed digit --- the pre-refactor source
through its original driver, the current source through that driver, and the current source
through the sweep runner --- so neither the implementation nor the harness is at fault. Two
further facts complete the picture, and together they are more useful than either alone.

The first is hardware. A cell of an unrelated grid happened to run the same configuration on the
same GPU \emph{model} as the original, and reproduced its entire twelve-point validation trace
to every printed digit, ending at $430.0$; the same configuration on the other device traces
$711.6, 640.3, 686.0, \dots$ and ends at $645.0$. The two already differ at the first evaluation
in the third significant figure ($711.70$ against $711.61$), so the split occurs within the first
500 steps and is then amplified to 215 perplexity. Nothing differs but the order of
floating-point reductions.

The second is the seed. Over three seeds on one device the undamped configuration gives
$610.9 / 408.8 / 367.5$, that is $\ReproUndampedMean \pm \ReproUndampedSd$: \textbf{a standard
deviation of \ReproUndampedPct\% of its own mean}. The earlier figure sits comfortably inside that range.

These are one phenomenon seen twice, and \Cref{fig:repro} shows it happening. In this configuration any perturbation at the last bit ---
a different reduction order, a different seed --- moves the outcome by hundreds of perplexity.
The earlier number was therefore both exactly reproducible on the hardware that produced it and
not a measurement of anything.

The same \ReproDampedN{} seeds with the label update damped ($\alpha_Z = 0.25$) give
$\ReproDampedMean \pm \ReproDampedSd$ --- \textbf{\ReproDampedPct\%} of its mean against
\ReproUndampedPct\%, a spread \ReproSdRatio{} times tighter. We draw two conclusions and apply
them throughout. No single-seed number from the undamped region is reported as a result here, and
comparisons in that region from the earlier work are withdrawn rather than repeated. The
largest of them read a global variable as improving an undamped $\nlab = 24$ model from
$321.4$ to $297.7$; that is $23.7$ perplexity on one seed each, against a spread of
$\ReproUndampedSd$ in the same region, and it is not a measurement. And the interventions
this paper is about should be judged on \emph{variance} as much as on the mean. Damping was
previously reported as an improvement from 641.5 to 248.3; it is at least as much an
improvement in reproducibility, and reproducibility is what lets any later comparison mean
anything at all. The damped record itself does replicate, and we say so as readily as we
withdraw the rest: re-run here from scratch it gives $253.0 \pm 6.0$ over three seeds against
the $250.7 \pm 2.3$ originally reported, well inside both spreads. Every headline number below
is a three-seed mean with its spread, and every grid cell carries a count of seeds that failed
to learn, because a mean taken over a mixture of learned and collapsed runs measures neither.

\paragraph{Scaling curves.}
\label{sec:exp-scaling}
\Cref{fig:scaling} draws validation perplexity against parameter count for all three models,
each point the best of its learning-rate search at a 15{,}000-step budget with
validation-selected checkpoints. Two features of the \emph{baselines} shape everything after.
First, the transformer's own curve is flat above roughly $2\times10^5$ non-embedding
parameters: 129.2 at $d_{\text{model}}=64$ against 124.7 at 160 and 125.3 at 256, with training
perplexity falling from 68.5 to 10.2 over the same range. On a corpus of 929{,}589 tokens the
baseline stops being parameter-limited and starts being data-limited, so a comparison run only
at the top of that range compares two models in a regime where one of them has stopped
improving. Second, at matched \emph{non-embedding} budget the looped transformer is
\emph{better} than the standard one below about $5\times10^4$ parameters (164.7 at 12{,}768
against 195.2 at 13{,}152) and worse above it (135.7 at 198{,}528 against 129.2 at 200{,}064).
At matched \emph{total} parameters the standard transformer wins everywhere. This was written
into the experiment's falsification criterion before it ran, and it revises the framing of
\Cref{tab:three-point}: at this scale weight sharing is not a handicap that the causal PT pays
for structure, it is a saving --- which makes the causal-PT-versus-looped comparison the
sharper one rather than the more forgiving one.

Against those curves the causal PT sits above, and the interesting question is how its
\emph{slope} compares. Fitting a power law to each Pareto envelope over its own measured range
gives $\OwnTf$ for the causal transformer, $\OwnLp$ for the looped one and $\OwnPt$ for the
causal PT with its head temperature corrected, which invites the reading that the correction has
restored the model's scaling exponent to a transformer's. \textbf{That reading is an artefact of
fitting each curve over a different range}, and the way it is wrong is worth stating: the
baselines extend to $1.2\times10^{6}$ non-embedding parameters, well into the regime where this
corpus and not the parameter count is binding, and those flat points drag their fitted slopes
down. Two exponents may only be compared if they were fitted
on the same window, and on this corpus that constraint bites hard: the looped transformer comes
out at $\ExpTfvLpLp$ over the transformer's range and $\ExpLpvPtLp$ over the causal PT's, from
the same runs. Three of the four curves --- transformer, looped, and the causal PT at low rank
--- are measured over one common range, \ExpTriLo{} to \ExpTriHi{} non-embedding parameters, and
that is where the comparison is made:

\textbf{Both differences are in the same direction, and the first is much the larger.} Sharing
parameters across depth costs $\ExpTriTf$ against $\ExpTriLp$ --- which is what
\Cref{tab:three-point} predicts, since the looped model has the sharing and none of the
structure --- and the syntactic structure costs a further $\ExpTriLp$ against $\ExpTriLow$.
Of the gap between the transformer's exponent and the causal PT's, then, roughly four fifths
opens with the sharing and one fifth with the structure. That split is a difference of fits over
four, three and eight points and should be read as an order of magnitude, not a measurement; the
ordering behind it is the robust part. In particular the causal PT does not scale \emph{like} a
looped transformer, which is the reading the earlier fits invited: it is shallower than one, by
a smaller but consistent margin.

The corrected-PT curve cannot join that table --- on the range where it, the transformer and the
looped model are all measured, the looped frontier has two points, and a power law through two
points is a line through two points. It enters instead through the pairwise comparisons in
\Cref{tab:exponents-full}, each fitted on the range its own two curves share, where the same
ordering holds in all five pairs and the two leave-one-out ranges are disjoint in every pair
that has enough points to compute one. What does \emph{not} appear anywhere in our conclusions
is a fit over each curve's own full range, which is what one would report by default and which
here reverses the reading entirely.

How much of the vertical offset the structure costs, as opposed to the slope, is what the switch
ladder below is for.

\Cref{tab:main} makes the budget comparison
directly. \textbf{The gap is smallest where the models are smallest and widens with budget},
which is what a shallower exponent means in practice: at \MBFirstNonemb{} non-embedding
parameters, the smallest budget at which a causal transformer was actually run, the causal PT is
within a factor $\MBFirstRatioGpt$ of one interpolated to the same budget, and by
\MBLastNonemb{} it is a factor $\MBLastRatioGpt$ behind. The looped transformer, which is
measured over the whole range, runs from $\MBFirstRatioLooped$ at the smallest point to
$\MBLastRatioLooped$ at the largest. The best absolute figure is $\PTbestVal \pm \PTbestSd$ at
$\nlab = \PTbestLab$, $\krank = \PTbestRank$; the comparison in our earlier work, made at one
point and at a very different budget, gave $2.17$.

Every causal PT entry in this paper is a mean over seeds, never the best of them. That is worth
saying because the two differ here by more than the differences we are discussing: at
$\nlab = \SpreadLab$, $\krank = \SpreadRank$ the best of \SpreadSeeds{} seeds gives $\SpreadBest$
and the mean $\SpreadMean \pm \SpreadSd$, and a paper that quoted the first while claiming
\SpreadSeeds{} seeds would be reporting a maximum and calling it a
measurement --- which, in a model whose seed spread reaches $130$ in the region we started
from, is exactly the failure this project set out to stop repeating.

\paragraph{Width transfer, and why the source temperature fails.}
\label{sec:exp-width}
\Cref{sec:temperature} predicted that Wu \& Tu's head temperature $\lamH = 1/\nlab$, being
calibrated for the uniform-belief limit, would make the label update's feedback loop worse as
the model is widened, so that any damping of that update must be strengthened with $\nlab$.
\Cref{fig:width}(a) is that prediction measured, and it is worth being
precise about how much of it the grid resolves. The undamped model is the worst cell at every
width, and at $\nlab \in \{32, 96, 128\}$ it sits on the unigram baseline ($700.7$, $698.1$ and
$701.4$ against $688.8$) with the collapse signature the mechanism predicts: the message
overwhelms the unary term and the label beliefs go flat, with a prefix-ablation KL of exactly
zero --- the model's prediction at slot $t$ stops depending on the prefix at all. But the best
damping sits at the smallest value we swept, $\alpha_Z = 0.125$, at five of the seven widths,
so the grid \emph{bounds} $\alpha^\star(\nlab)$ rather than locating it. What it establishes is
that damping is necessary at the source temperature and that the necessity does not lift as
$\nlab$ grows; the functional form of $\alpha^\star(\nlab)$ would need a sweep that goes below
$0.125$, which we did not run.

The consequence is visible in the ladders of \Cref{tab:main}. Carrying the constants tuned at
$\nlab = 32$ across the width axis --- which is what one does in the absence of a rule ---
reproduces our earlier record at that width ($253.0 \pm 6.0$ against $250.7 \pm 2.3$) and then
degrades, to $287.4 \pm 52.5$ at $\nlab=48$ and $470.0 \pm 160.7$ at $\nlab=64$. The means matter
less than the standard deviations: the ladder does not gently worsen, it becomes unpredictable.

\Cref{fig:mechanism} shows why, within a run, and it separates two failures that a
final perplexity conflates: at $\nlab = 96$ the beliefs never leave the uniform point at all,
while at $\nlab = 32$ they sharpen to $5.5$, hold for two thousand steps and then collapse back
to uniform. Both end at the unigram baseline; only the second is the runaway the mechanism
predicts, and only a within-run trace tells them apart.

\Cref{fig:width}(b) is the repair. Standardising the head logits over the head domain before
the softmax --- so that the attention pattern is invariant to the overall scale of the arc
scores, and only their relative pattern selects --- revives widths that are entirely dead under
the source temperature, \emph{with no damping at all}: at $\alpha_Z = 1$, $\nlab = 32$ goes from
700.7 to $282.2 \pm 6.6$ over three seeds and $\nlab = 64$ from a collapse to $254.0 \pm 1.9$.
Run as a full ladder at the baselines' budget it reaches $229.8 \pm 8.5$ at $\nlab = 48$ and
$219.8 \pm 4.2$ at $\nlab = 64$, against $287.4 \pm 52.5$ and $470.0 \pm 160.7$ for the control.

\textbf{The gain transfers; the width limit does not disappear.} Sweeping the standardisation
gain over $\{1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6\}$ at four widths with three seeds each, the argmin is $1$ at
every width, and the working range is the same $[1,2]$ at every width (\Cref{tab:gain}) --- as the mechanism
predicts, since after standardisation the attention entropy depends on the gain and on the
length of the head domain, not on $\nlab$. Above the range the failure is sharp and
reproducible rather than noisy: at $\nlab=32$, gain $3$ collapses on $3/3$ seeds
($700.5 \pm 0.7$). But the correction does not remove the ceiling, it moves it: at
$\nlab \in \{96, 128\}$ the rule ladder becomes unstable again ($372 \pm 277$, $509 \pm 187$,
with one collapsed seed each). The usable width roughly doubles; it does not become unbounded,
and we say so rather than reporting only the widths where it works.

\textbf{Learning-rate transfer is not what changes.} We also ran the diagnostic that maximal
update parametrisation is judged by \citep{yang2022tensor} and that \citet{kuang2026scaling}
apply to the encoder: validation perplexity against learning rate at four widths, under both
temperatures. The argmin moves by one step of a $2\times$ grid over an $8\times$ range of width
in \emph{both} arms ($2\times10^{-2} \to 10^{-2}$ under the source temperature,
$4\times10^{-2} \to 2\times10^{-2}$ under the standardised one; \Cref{tab:lr}). So the two results are about
different things, and we do not claim ours as a substitute for theirs: what the temperature fixes
is not where the optimal learning rate sits, but whether the model learns at all at a given
width.

\textbf{The Kruskal rank inverts the configuration.} The arc scores are decomposed as
$\Tfac{c} = \Ufac{c}\Vfac{c}{}^{\!\top}$ with $\Ufac{c},\Vfac{c} \in \R^{\nlab\times\krank}$, and
every configuration in this literature sets $\krank = \nlab$, which is the most expensive setting
of the decomposition. It is also not the best one: at $\nlab = 96$, $\krank = 48$ gives
$242.6 \pm 3.3$ against $\krank = 96$'s $275.6 \pm 27.1$ --- better perplexity at half the arc
parameters and an eighth of the seed spread --- and $\krank = 12$ gives $257.8 \pm 4.5$ at
18{,}624 non-embedding parameters, eight times smaller and still ahead of the best $\nlab = 32$
configuration at a comparable budget. The reason is structural: the arc score reaches inference
only contracted against a belief, $\Bfac{c}_{j,a} = \E_{\qbar{j}}[\Tfac{c}_{a,\cdot}]$, so what
is usable is its rank on the affine hull of the reachable beliefs (\Cref{tab:rank}), and those are measurably
concentrated.

Run as a full ladder at the baselines' budget, low rank does two things at once. The curve sits
uniformly \emph{below} the $\krank = \nlab$ one --- $223.1 \pm 5.6$ at 18{,}624 non-embedding
parameters against $372 \pm 277$ for $\krank = 96$ at 147{,}648 --- and \textbf{the width ceiling
moves}: where both earlier ladders became unstable at $\nlab \geq 96$, this one trains stably
through $\nlab = \LadderTopLab$ at $\krank = \LadderTopRank$
($\LadderTopVal \pm \LadderTopSd$), with seed spreads of $\LadderSdLo$ to $\LadderSdHi$ across
all \LadderNok{} configurations that trained. It does not disappear: at
$\nlab = 256$, $\krank = 64$ --- the widest and highest-rank cell we ran --- one seed in three
still lands on the unigram, which is why that row of \Cref{tab:main} carries a collapse count
and no perplexity. The rank is what buys the extra width, and the same ceiling is waiting above
it. Nothing in the source prescribes $\krank = \nlab$;
it is what Wu \& Tu's Table 2 rule $\min(64,\nlab)$ produces at every width used here, and it
was carried along unexamined --- by us as much as by anyone.

\textbf{We report what this repair costs, because it is not free.} A query-dependent temperature
is still a temperature, and $\lamH$ is a free per-variable message weight in the source, so
dividing by $\|\QZ{i}\|_2\sqrt{\nlab}$ --- which is $1$ exactly at the uniform belief --- stays
inside the family of choices the construction already makes. Standardising the logits does not:
it is a normalisation applied to the messages, outside the update equations, and it forfeits the
property that every update is the centred gradient of a fixed per-slot free energy. It is, in
other words, precisely the kind of operation \Cref{sec:variants} lists as a tripwire, and it is
the one that works. We regard that as a finding rather than an embarrassment --- it prices one
specific consequence of the factor-graph constraint, and it is the same price a transformer pays
knowingly when it puts a layer normalisation in front of its attention. The variational reading
survives for the content stream and the readout; it is the head-update temperature alone that
steps outside.

\paragraph{Initialisation, and the global variables.}
\label{sec:exp-ablations}
\citet{wu2023probabilistic} propose global variables in their Appendix B.3 as the in-graph
substitute for a feed-forward block and never test them. We do, and the first thing to report is
a correction to our own earlier account. That account said the head dies under ``uniform''
initialisation; the word meant the \emph{treatment} --- one scale and one $L_2$ coefficient for
every factor --- and not the distribution, which has always been Gaussian. The mechanism is a
symmetry: with the rows of $\Bglob$ close together relative to their own length the head
posterior is uniform, the message is the row mean, and every row then receives the identical
gradient $1/m$, so nothing separates them; a shared $L_2$ shrinks the spread that would.

Where the answer comes from also needs saying. We first put the distribution question directly
--- Gaussian against a matched-variance uniform and a semi-orthogonal draw --- in the source
configuration at one seed per cell, and it cannot be read: at $\nlab=32$ the three draws give
$700.7$, $375.0$ and $386.3$, differences of three hundred perplexity in a region whose seed
standard deviation is $\ReproUndampedSd$. That grid is \Cref{tab:init} and no
conclusion is drawn from it. What follows is the same question re-asked at three seeds in the
configuration \Cref{sec:exp-width} selects, which is the only region where an initialisation
effect of the size anyone cares about would be visible at all.

Measured twice, in two configurations, the head answers two different questions --- and the
second answer withdraws the first. Under the \emph{source} head temperature at $\nlab = 32$,
where the model collapses, the configuration without the head sits on the unigram ($700.7$)
while several with it land between $323$ and $380$: a second message into $\Zv{t}$ that does not
pass through the arc factors evidently breaks the runaway loop, and on that evidence the head
looks load-bearing. Re-run at the same width in the configuration \Cref{sec:exp-width}
selects --- standardised temperature, undamped, three seeds --- \textbf{the effect is gone}:
without the head, $243.2 \pm 5.3$; with it, across three initialisation scales and both $L_2$
treatments, between $242.5$ and $254.1$, none of them distinguishable from no head at all
against those spreads.

So at $\nlab = 32$ the head was not adding capacity, it was rescuing a pathology, and the
temperature correction removes the pathology more cheaply.

At $\nlab = 64$ the answer is neither the same nor its opposite, and the shape of it is worth
setting out because it is what a practitioner will actually meet. That width sits at the edge of
the stable region: without the head, one of two seeds lands on the unigram ($216.8$ and
$704.1$). With it, four of the six treatments train on every seed and reach $221$ to $234$ ---
better than anything at $\nlab = 32$ --- while two do not, and the two that fail are not the two
one would guess: the smallest initialisation outside the shared $L_2$ ($224.0$, $616.5$,
$639.1$) and the middle one inside it ($215.0$, $235.2$, $667.0$). \textbf{The head does not
make the model stable; it changes which configurations are.} The largest initialisation we ran,
$0.5$, is stable under both $L_2$ treatments, which is the one regularity in the block and the
opposite of what a shrink-toward-symmetry account would predict.

What does hold at both widths, in every cell of the grid, is the diagnostic: $H(Q_g)$ is between
$0.93$ and $1.000$ of its maximum everywhere, including in the cells where the head is carrying
more message than the word unary and in the cells where it is associated with the model training
at all. Whatever the head is doing at $\nlab = 64$, it is not doing it by discriminating between
its own values. The diagnostic says the same thing from inside: the head
posterior's entropy is between $0.94$ and $1.000$ of its maximum in every cell of the grid, so
what the head sends is a near-constant label prior and not the feed-forward-like operator it is
proposed as --- including in the cells where its message is \emph{larger} than the word unary
($1.19$ to $1.52$ times, outside the shared $L_2$). Perplexity alone cannot tell a uniform
posterior from an informative one, which is why \Cref{tab:globalhead} reports the entropy beside
it. On Prof.\ Tu's question directly: Gaussian initialisation at three scales spanning
$25\times$, with and without the head in the shared $L_2$, changes nothing that matters.
\textbf{The distribution, which is the question as it was asked.} Having distinguished the
treatment from the draw, we varied the draw as well. At $\nlab=64$ with $\nglob=64$ head values,
at initialisation scale $0.02$ inside the shared $L_2$ --- the one treatment at which all three
draws were run --- Gaussian gives $225.8$ over three seeds, a semi-orthogonal draw $219.5$ over
two, and a matched-variance uniform draw $249.1$ over two, with one of those two seeds at
$272.9$. \textbf{The orthogonal draw sits inside the Gaussian cell's own seed range}
($214.1$ to $233.3$), so on this evidence the draw does not matter, with a hint that the uniform
one is worse.

We registered a sharper prediction before running this and it is not tested by what we ran, so
we say that rather than claiming the support: the prediction was that an orthogonal $\Bglob$
would survive a shared $L_2$ that kills a small-scale Gaussian one, and in this configuration
the small-scale Gaussian draw does not die under the shared $L_2$ --- it dies \emph{outside} it.
The grid never put the prediction at risk.

The capacity axis says the same thing from another direction. Quartering the head, from
$\nglob=64$ values to $16$, costs nothing ($224.2$ against $222.2$, three seeds each), which is
what a posterior that stays uniform implies: more values to spread the same constant over.

\paragraph{What the construction costs: the switch ladder.}
\label{sec:exp-switches}
\Cref{tab:switches} prices each of the ten differences twice --- once as its cost in an
otherwise ordinary decoder (S1), once as the benefit of restoring it inside the all-PT rung
(S2). The S1 column is the one to read first, and it is largely good news for the construction:
\textbf{three of the ten differences are free}, and two of those three are the ones this model
class is most often criticised for. Tying the value to the key, which the factor graph forces
because both roles are the same message through the same arc factor, costs $-0.6$; relative
positions cost $-0.4$; removing the query projection costs $1.9$. Layer normalisation costs nothing
measurable here once each rung is tuned --- at a single learning rate it appeared to cost
$18.5$, and that was the rate, not the switch. What does cost is the feed-forward block
($15.1$) and weight sharing ($12.5$), neither of which is specific to this construction.

Two rungs are not costs but cliffs --- the readout and the simplex state, at $+558$ and $+564$
--- and they are the two the construction is most answerable for. The second is discussed below,
because its number does not mean what it appears to. The first does. Giving an otherwise
ordinary decoder the mixture readout of \Cref{sec:output} in place
of its linear head --- at matched width, with the embeddings tied as the factor graph requires
--- does not train at all: $686.8$ against a unigram of $688.8$, with a \emph{training}
perplexity of $736.6$, and the same at every learning rate we ran it at ($10^{-3}$ to
$10^{-2}$). That is the evaluation-time swap of \Cref{sec:exp3} arrived at from the opposite
direction: there, weights trained for one readout are useless under the other; here, a readout
transplanted into a model built around the other cannot be trained in the first place. The
mixture readout is not a drop-in for a linear head, and neither experiment on its own would
establish that.

\textbf{The ladder is one seed per cell}, unlike every other result in this paper, because
$128$ cells at three seeds was not affordable. Differences of a point or two in the S1 column
should therefore be read as ``not distinguishable from zero'' rather than as small effects with
a sign; the ones we draw conclusions from --- the three free switches against the $12$--$15$ of
the feed-forward block and weight sharing, and the cliff at the simplex state --- are larger
than the seed spread we measure elsewhere in the same regime.

The exception is the simplex constraint on the state, at $+564$, and it should not be read as
``the simplex costs 564 perplexity''. A transformer whose state is passed through a softmax
after every block has a state norm in $[\nlab^{-1/2}, 1]$ while its attention still divides by
$\sqrt{d_{\text{head}}}$ --- the mis-calibration of \Cref{sec:temperature}, arrived at from the
transformer side. The ladder has no temperature switch, so flipping the state alone changes the
geometry without changing the temperature that geometry requires, and the interaction between
two differences lands on one of them. The real decoder, which is simplex-constrained and has a
temperature suited to it, reaches $\LadderTopVal$. \Cref{fig:ladder} draws the cumulative
ladder, where that interaction is visible as a spike the last flip partly undoes.

\paragraph{Two knobs with no gentle regime.}

\paragraph{Inference depth buys nothing, and costs.}
\label{sec:exp-depth}
The number of mean-field iterations $\niter$ is the causal PT's depth: it is what makes the
model a weight-shared stack rather than a single layer, and it is the axis it shares with a
Looped transformer, so \Cref{tab:three-point}'s framing depends on knowing what it is worth.
Wu \& Tu's Table 2 fixes $\niter = 3$ and neither paper varies it inside a configuration that
trains. Varying it here, one seed per cell in the damped configuration at $\nlab = 32$ where the
seed spread is $\ReproDampedSd$: $\niter = 1$ gives $241.3$, $2$ gives $246.5$, $3$ gives
$248.3$ and $8$ gives $275.3$. The predictive rounds $\tau$ behave the same way, $242.1$ at
$\tau=1$ against $248.3$ at $\tau=2$ --- the same $248.3$, because the base configuration is
$\niter=3, \tau=2$ and both sweeps pass through it.

\textbf{Perplexity increases monotonically with inference depth.} The step from $\niter=1$ to
$\niter=3$ is about one seed standard deviation and we do not read it alone; the span to
$\niter=8$, $34$ perplexity, is five of them and the monotonicity across four points is not an
accident of one cell. Two readings are available and we cannot separate them here. Either the
extra iterations are genuinely harmful --- each one re-injects the same arc factors, and
\Cref{sec:exp-width} showed exactly that loop running away when its gain is mis-set --- or they
are harmless but the optimisation problem they create is worse. Either way the practical
statement is the same and it is uncomfortable for the analogy the model is built on: at this
scale, \emph{the causal PT is best as a one-iteration model}, which is to say as a model whose
looped character is vestigial. That is a further reason not to read the shallow scaling slope of
\Cref{sec:exp-scaling} as evidence about depth-shared computation.

\paragraph{Exact vs.\ mean-field readout.}
\label{sec:exp3}
Two measurements are needed and they answer different questions: swapping the readout at
evaluation on the same trained weights isolates the approximation error, while training under
each measures the end-to-end effect. The swap is computed for every causal PT cell in this paper
at the cost of one extra pass, and it is meaningful only at $\lamW = 1$ --- any other value makes
it measure a temperature (\Cref{app:message-weights}). \Cref{app:worked-example} shows the two
readouts differing concretely on one slot.

The swap is measured for every causal PT cell in this paper, and it gives a clear negative
result: \textbf{the two readouts are not interchangeable at evaluation.} Substituting
\Cref{eq:exact-readout} for the mean-field readout on mean-field-trained weights costs between
a factor of $\SwapMildestRatio$ and a factor of $\SwapWorstRatio$ in validation perplexity, at
every one of the \SwapNconfigs{} $(\nlab, \krank)$ configurations for which we have three
uncollapsed seeds: from $\SwapMildestTrained$ to $\SwapMildestSwapped$ at its mildest
($\nlab = \SwapMildestLab$, $\krank = \SwapMildestRank$) and from $\SwapWorstTrained$ to
$\SwapWorstSwapped$ at its worst ($\nlab = \SwapWorstLab$, $\krank = \SwapWorstRank$). Both
sides of each comparison are seed means. Inspecting a trained model, the
exact readout's logits are not degenerate; they simply discriminate less between words at a
fixed context (standard deviation $1.63$ against the mean-field readout's $2.64$) and more
between contexts for a fixed word ($2.73$ against $2.42$). Parameters trained so that one
readout is accurate do not make the other accurate, which is unsurprising once stated but is not
what a reader would assume from a construction that presents the two as alternative inferences
in one model.

The practical consequence is that the cheap half of this comparison does not substitute for the
expensive half: the readouts have to be compared by \emph{training} under each. Doing so, in the
damped configuration at $\nlab=32$ where the seed spread is $\ReproDampedSd$, gives $348.8$
under the exact readout against $248.3$ under the mean-field one --- the same model, the same
loop, the same budget, differing only in which readout the loss is taken through.

\textbf{The richer family is worse.} \Cref{prop:readout}(iii) says the exact readout's image
strictly contains the mean-field readout's, and it costs no parameters, so the only way it can
lose by a hundred perplexity is that the parameters realising the better family are not the ones
this optimiser finds. We think the reason is visible in the form: the exact readout reaches the
vocabulary through $\LSE_{j}$ over the head domain, which is a maximum in disguise and passes
gradient almost entirely to the single best-scoring position, while the mean-field readout
reaches it through $\Sfac\QZ{t}$, an average that passes gradient to every label in proportion
to its belief. A strictly larger hypothesis class that is harder to search is a familiar
outcome, and it is worth reporting plainly because the construction's own logic points the other
way: the exact readout is what the graph says the answer is, and the approximation to it trains
better. \TODO{the $\nlab=16$ pair, and whether the ordering holds undamped.}

\paragraph{Factored labels.}
\label{sec:exp-factored}
The construction of \Cref{sec:variants} predicts an interaction rather than a main effect:
factoring the label variable should buy nothing under the mean-field readout, because the logits
stay affine in the concatenated beliefs, and whatever it buys should appear only through the
exact readout. The structural half is already confirmed: at $\nlab = 8$ the affine
dimension of the log-conditionals is $8$, $7$ and $5$ for $K = 1, 2, 4$ under the mean-field
readout --- never above $\nlab$, whatever $K$ is, which is the prediction --- and strictly
above $\nlab$ under the exact readout on the same contexts. \Cref{app:factored} gives the
parameter accounting, which the source states incorrectly.
